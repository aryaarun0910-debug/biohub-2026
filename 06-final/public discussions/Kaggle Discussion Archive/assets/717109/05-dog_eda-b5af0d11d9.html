🔵 Why the DoG Baseline Works So Well

The Kaggle rule-based baseline uses no learning at all: it detects nuclei with a multi-scale Difference-of-Gaussians (DoG) blob filter, links them frame-to-frame with the Hungarian algorithm, and closes 1-frame gaps. All figures below are the baseline's actual DoG detector run on our data.

1. What DoG is — a band-pass filter shaped like a nucleus

A nucleus in the microscope is essentially a bright Gaussian blob of a characteristic size. DoG subtracts a large-σ blur (the local background) from a small-σ blur (which keeps the blob). The result is a filter that is maximal exactly at the centre of a blob whose size sits between the two σ's and near-zero everywhere else. This is the ideal, prior-perfect matched filter for nuclei — which is why it beats a generic intensity threshold and needs no training data.

2. The seven design choices that make it strong

  1. Physical-unit σ (µm), made isotropic. Voxels are anisotropic (1.625 × 0.406 × 0.406 µm). The code XY-downsamples by 4 so the grid becomes ~isotropic, then sets σ in microns (σ/eff). A spherical nucleus stays spherical → the blob filter is correct in real space.
  2. Multi-scale DoG (scales = [[1.5, 4.0], [2.2, 5.5]] µm): takes the max over two DoG responses, so both smaller and larger nuclei fire. Robust to cell-size variation over development.
  3. Robust per-frame normalization (1st–99.7th percentile): rescales each frame independently, revealing dim nuclei a global threshold would miss. Directly boosts recall.
  4. Physical-space NMS via an ellipsoidal ball footprint of radius min_distance = 3.2 µm — one peak per nucleus, tuned to real cell spacing. (Exactly the lesson we learned: too-large a radius collapses detections.)
  5. Dual threshold: DoG response ≥ rel_threshold and raw intensity above the median. Kills background/noise peaks.
  6. Over-detect to the true count (max_peaks = 40,000): keeps thousands of blobs, matching the real ~25k–65k cells — the GT is sparse, so this is rewarded, not penalised.
  7. Sub-voxel refine + gap closing: intensity-weighted centre-of-mass sharpens each centre; close_gaps bridges 1-frame detection misses (all GT edges are dt=1, so one bridge yields two matchable edges) — a direct edge-recall win.

4. Parameter EDA — why the chosen values are good

Left: lowering rel_threshold raises recall but explodes the count; the baseline's 0.045 sits at the knee. Right: the NMS radius is the critical knob — past ~4 µm the detector starts merging neighbouring nuclei and both count and recall fall. This is the same failure that zeroed our early scores when NMS was set to 50 µm.

What it does NOT solve — divisions. The final config sets allow_divisions = False: the baseline skips mitosis entirely and banks the edge term. Divisions are rare (0.11%) and structural, so a rule-based linker gains little there. That is exactly where a learned model has headroom — and where the extra 0.1× division weight is still on the table.
Takeaway for our pipeline: adopt DoG's winning ingredients — physical-isotropic scale, multi-scale band-pass, robust per-frame normalization, 3 µm-class NMS, over-detection to the true count, and gap closing — as either a strong standalone detector or a teacher/target for the learned model, then spend the learning capacity on the part DoG can't do: divisions.
